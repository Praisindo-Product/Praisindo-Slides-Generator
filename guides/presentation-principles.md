# Presentation principles

How the best decks are built: the storyline conventions of top-tier strategy and Big 4 consulting
practice, and the research on how people take in slides (cognitive load, multimedia learning,
graphical perception, legibility). For an agent writing a Praisindo deck. Read it before the outline,
and come back to it at each judgement call.

These are principles, not templates. They say what works and why, so you can decide. A deck may break
any of them when it has a reason that serves the audience better; say what you did and why when you
hand it over. What is fixed (the shell, the lockup, the design system's look) is in
`slide-guidelines.md`; this guide is about everything inside the frame.

Professional style, always: calm, precise, evidence first, no hype, no decoration for its own sake.

## 1. Start from the brief

A deck is an argument for a particular audience in a particular setting. Settle these before writing a
slide; ask only what you cannot work out from the request and the material (`workflow.md` lists the
questions to ask).

| Question | Why it changes the deck |
| --- | --- |
| What should the audience decide, believe or do after it? | This is the governing thought. Every slide either supports it or goes to the appendix. |
| Who is in the room, and what do they already know? | Experts need less explanation and tolerate more density (the expertise reversal effect, section 4); mixed audiences need the executive summary to stand alone. |
| Is it presented in a room or read on a device? | It sets type size, density and how much the slides must explain without a speaker (section 6). |
| How long is the slot? | At one to two minutes a slide, the slot sets the length of the main story. Everything else is appendix. |
| Is there a reference to follow, and how strictly? | Strict restyling and an improved storyline are different jobs (section 11). |
| Is a speaker script wanted? | The script carries what the slide leaves out (section 10). |

## 2. The storyline

### Answer first

Lead with the answer, then support it. Busy senior readers decide in the first minutes whether to keep
listening; a deck that builds slowly to its conclusion loses them. This is the core of Barbara Minto's
Pyramid Principle, the basis of storylining at McKinsey and since at most strategy and Big 4 firms.

- **Governing thought.** One sentence that answers the audience's question. If you cannot write it, the
  deck is not ready to build.
- **Key line.** Three to five supporting points directly below it, each a full sentence. These usually
  become the executive summary and the section titles.
- **Support.** Each key point is backed by evidence: data, examples, the product itself.
- **MECE.** Points at the same level are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive: no overlap, no
  gap. Overlap confuses; a gap invites the question you did not answer.
- **Grouping.** Group supporting points either deductively (a chain: rule, case, conclusion) or
  inductively (parallel reasons of the same kind). Inductive groups are easier to follow in a room.

### SCQA for the opening

Situation (what the audience accepts as true), Complication (what changed or what is at stake),
Question (what that raises), Answer (the governing thought). It frames the executive summary and the
first minutes of the talk. Keep the situation short; the audience already knows it.

For a persuasive deck, Nancy Duarte's structure alternates "what is" with "what could be" and ends on
a call to action. Use either; both put the audience's problem before the solution's features.

### Horizontal and vertical logic

- **Horizontal logic:** read only the slide titles, in order. They should tell the whole story as a
  paragraph. If they do not, fix the storyline before fixing any slide.
- **Vertical logic:** everything on a slide supports its title, and nothing else. A fact that does not
  support the title moves to another slide or the appendix.

### Ghost deck first

Before designing anything, write the outline as a ghost deck: one line per slide with the action title
and a note of the evidence that will prove it (a chart of X, a table of Y, the product screen Z). Test
the horizontal logic on the ghost deck. Show it to the person for a long deck or a new story; it is the
cheapest moment to change direction.

### Main story and appendix

- The main story carries what the meeting needs: the answer, the reasons, the proof, and what happens
  next. The rest is appendix, labelled and indexed (A1, A2...) so it can be found during questions.
- Moving a fact to the appendix is not leaving it out. Dropping a fact is; say so when you do.
- End with the ask: the decision needed, the next steps, and who does what by when. If owners or dates
  are not known, say what has to be agreed rather than inventing them.

### Typical shapes

These are common shapes, not required ones; choose what the material calls for.

- **Proposal or pitch:** executive summary; the client's need or opportunity; the solution; how it
  works; proof (case, demo, figures); why us; plan and commercial terms if any; next steps.
- **Decision paper or steering committee:** decision needed; recommendation; options compared on the
  same criteria; risks and mitigations; plan; the ask.
- **Progress review:** status at a glance (on track, at risk, off track); what was done; what is next;
  issues and decisions needed.
- **Product overview:** what it is in one sentence; who it is for; what changes for them; how it works;
  proof; roadmap.

## 3. The slide

### One message per slide

Each slide makes one point. Two points make two slides.

### Action titles

The title is the point, as a full sentence the audience could repeat: "Settlement fails fell 40% after
the pre-check", not "Settlement fails". This is the assertion of the assertion-evidence approach
(Michael Alley), which in controlled studies improved audience comprehension over topic-title-and-bullet
slides (Garner and Alley 2013). It is also the "so what" consulting firms require of every title.

- One or two lines; about 70 characters fits one line at the engine's title size.
- Specific: numbers, names, direction. "Three integration choices to settle before design" beats
  "Next steps".
- True to the evidence below it; never claim more than the slide shows.
- Section names and labels go in the overline, not the title.

### Evidence, not decoration

The body proves the title: a chart, a table, a diagram, a product screen, a short structured list.
Prefer a visual when the evidence is a relationship, a trend, a comparison or a flow. Prefer text when
the evidence is a set of discrete statements. A paragraph is rarely the best evidence on a slide.

### The glance test

A slide's point should be clear in about three seconds (Duarte's glance test). Look at the slide for
three seconds: if you cannot say what it claims, the title is weak or the body competes with it.

### Density follows the setting

- **Room:** the speaker carries the explanation; the slide carries the claim and the evidence. Few
  words, large type, one visual idea.
- **Device or pre-read:** the slide must explain itself, because nobody is there to explain it. More
  words are acceptable, in full sentences, still one point per slide, still scannable.

## 4. How people take in slides (the research)

Use these findings to make judgement calls. Effects vary with the audience and the material; they are
tendencies, not laws.

- **Working memory is small.** People hold about four chunks of new information at once (Cowan 2001;
  the older "seven plus or minus two" overstates it). Group items into a few chunks, and keep a list
  that must be held in mind to about four items, or structure it so it need not be held.
- **Cognitive load.** Learning suffers when effort goes into processing the presentation rather than the
  content (Sweller's cognitive load theory). Remove extraneous load: decoration, inconsistent layout,
  making the reader search for what goes with what.
- **Coherence.** Leave out interesting but irrelevant words, pictures and sounds; they reduce learning
  (Mayer's coherence principle). Every element must earn its place.
- **Signalling.** Cue the structure and the important parts: action titles, highlighting the one bar
  that matters, a tracker showing where you are in the story.
- **Redundancy.** Narration plus identical on-screen text is worse than narration plus a visual. Do not
  read paragraphs off the slide; put the paragraph in the script and a visual or a short claim on the
  slide. Short key phrases next to a visual are fine.
- **Spatial contiguity.** Put words next to what they describe: label chart lines directly instead of
  using a distant legend; put callouts on the screenshot, not below it.
- **Temporal contiguity and segmenting.** Show the visual while you say the words about it, and break a
  complex process into steps the audience can follow one at a time (one slide per stage, or a build).
- **Pre-training.** Define key terms or components before the process that uses them (introduce the
  three pillars before the fourteen flows).
- **Multimedia and dual coding.** Words with relevant pictures are understood and remembered better than
  words alone (Mayer's multimedia principle; Paivio's dual coding theory), and pictures are remembered
  better than words (the picture superiority effect). The picture must carry meaning; a stock photo does
  not.
- **Personalisation.** A conversational register ("you", "your teams") helps more than a formal,
  impersonal one. Keep it professional.
- **Expertise reversal.** What helps novices can hinder experts (Kalyuga and colleagues 2003). Senior
  specialists (heads of custody, operations, risk) want the dense, precise version; explaining basics to
  them costs attention and credibility. Match depth to the audience, and push basics to the appendix.

## 5. Numbers and charts

### Choose the chart from the message

Gene Zelazny's method: decide the comparison the title makes, then the chart follows.

| The message compares | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Parts of a whole | A single stacked bar or a 100% bar; a pie only for two or three parts |
| Items ranked | Horizontal bars, sorted |
| Change over time | Columns for few periods, a line for many |
| Distribution | A histogram or a column chart of ranges |
| Correlation | A scatter plot, or paired bars |
| Steps that add up | A waterfall |
| A flow or process | A diagram or a step track, not a chart |

### Encode with what people judge accurately

Cleveland and McGill (1984) ranked how accurately people judge quantities: position on a common scale
first, then position on unaligned scales, then length, angle and slope, area, volume, and colour
saturation last. So prefer bars and dots on a shared axis; avoid comparisons by area, angle or colour
shade when precision matters.

### Make the point visible

- Highlight what the title claims (the one bar, the one line, the one cell) in the accent or brand
  colour and keep the rest neutral.
- Label directly; drop legends when you can; drop gridlines that do not help reading values.
- Bars start at zero. Lines may start elsewhere when the axis says so.
- Remove ink that carries no data (Tufte's data-ink ratio). Note the nuance: memorable, meaningful
  embellishment can help recall without hurting accuracy (Bateman and colleagues 2010). Decoration that
  means something may stay; decoration that means nothing goes.

### Figures you can trust

- State units, currency, period and basis on the slide ("IDR bn, 31 Oct 2026, illustrative").
- Round to what the decision needs (two or three significant figures in a room), and keep the precise
  figure in the appendix or the table.
- Right-align numbers that are compared, with the same decimals.
- Check every total, share and difference; a single wrong sum costs the whole deck its credibility.
- Mark illustrative, estimated and preliminary figures as such. Cite the source of every external
  figure in a source line at the foot of the slide.

## 6. Type and legibility, by setting

The engine's canvas is 960 by 540 points, which is exactly PowerPoint's widescreen slide (13.33 by 7.5
inches); a point here is a point in PowerPoint.

### Why size depends on the room

Reading is fluent once text is large enough in the eye: vision research puts the threshold (critical
print size) at about 0.2 degrees of visual angle for the x-height, and reading stays fluent well above
it (Legge and Bigelow 2011). For a sans serif like Libre Franklin that gives a working estimate:

- **Minimum** size in slide points = 540 x D / (150 x H)
- **Comfortable** size in slide points = 540 x D / (100 x H)

where D is the distance to the farthest viewer and H is the height of the projected image, in the same
unit. Examples:

| Setting | D | H | Minimum | Comfortable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small meeting room, 65 inch screen | 3.5 m | 0.81 m | 16 pt | 23 pt |
| Boardroom, 85 inch screen | 6 m | 1.06 m | 20 pt | 31 pt |
| Training room, 2 m projection | 9 m | 1.7 m | 19 pt | 29 pt |

Projection and video standards (AVIXA DISCAS; the older 4-6-8 rule of thumb) size screens the same
way: from the farthest viewer and the smallest element that must be read. When the room is unknown,
assume a boardroom.

### Working sizes

| | Room (projected or shared screen) | Device (laptop, tablet, pre-read) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body text | 18 pt or more; 16 pt in a small room | 10 to 12 pt (the design system's scale) |
| Labels, table text, chart labels | 14 pt or more | 8.5 pt or more |
| Footnotes and sources (not meant to be read aloud) | 10 pt or more | 7.5 pt or more |
| Words on a content slide | About 40 or fewer | As many as the slide needs to stand alone, in full sentences |
| Table | About 6 rows by 4 columns | About 15 short rows |

The shell's title (22 pt) and frame are fixed and read well in both settings. Inside the frame, room
decks scale the body up; device decks use the design system's scale. If the content cannot fit at room
size, that is the signal to split the slide or move detail to the appendix, not to shrink the type.

### Typography

- Sentence case for titles and labels; capitals only for acronyms. All-caps text reads slower.
- Lines of about 50 to 75 characters for running text; shorter in a room.
- Left-align text; centre only short labels. Never justify on a slide.
- Two weights do most of the work (regular and semibold); hierarchy by size and weight, not by colour
  alone.

## 7. Colour

- Colour has a job: brand, emphasis, or meaning (status, series). Use the design system's tokens and
  the client's colours from the deck context.
- One accent per slide for emphasis; neutral for everything else.
- Keep meaning consistent across the deck: the same colour for the same series or status everywhere.
- About 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European descent have a colour vision deficiency, most
  often red-green. Never carry meaning by colour alone: add a label, a position or an icon.
- Contrast: at least 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for chart marks and large text against their background
  (WCAG 2.2). Projectors wash colours out, so a room deck needs more contrast than the screen suggests;
  avoid pale tints for anything that must be read.

## 8. Layout

- **Grid and alignment.** Align every element to the slide's content width and a few shared edges.
  Misalignment reads as carelessness even when nobody can say why.
- **Proximity.** Things that belong together sit together; space separates groups (Gestalt principles of
  proximity, similarity and common region). Prefer space to boxes and lines.
- **Reading order.** Title, then the main visual, then supporting text, left to right and top to bottom.
  Place the evidence where the eye lands after the title.
- **White space.** Room for the point to stand out. A slide full to the edges is a slide with no
  emphasis. Large empty areas are a different problem: enlarge the evidence or merge the slide.
- **Consistency.** The same kind of content in the same place on every slide, so the audience learns the
  layout once and then reads only the content.

## 9. Consulting conventions

The practices strategy and Big 4 consulting decks share. They exist because senior clients read many
decks; follow them unless the material gives a reason not to.

- **Executive summary up front**, answer first, standing on its own for someone who reads only that page.
- **Action titles** on every content slide; the titles alone tell the story.
- **Tracker.** In a long deck, an overline or small marker showing the section; the engine's overline
  does this.
- **Stickers.** "Preliminary", "Illustrative", "For discussion", "Draft" where they apply. Never present
  a draft or an illustration as final.
- **Source lines and footnotes** at the foot of a slide for every external figure and every assumption.
- **Dot-dash structure** for text: a short statement, with at most one level of support below it. Never
  three levels of bullets.
- **Parallel structure.** Items in a list share a grammatical form and a level of detail.
- **Options on the same criteria.** When comparing options, use the same criteria for each and say which
  is recommended and why.
- **Risks with mitigations.** A risk without a mitigation or an owner is a worry, not a plan.
- **Next steps with owners and dates.** If they are not yet known, list what has to be agreed.
- **Appendix** for backup, indexed so a question can be answered with a slide number.
- **Pre-read versus presentation.** A deck sent ahead may be denser and self-explanatory; the deck
  projected in the meeting is lighter. When both are needed, the appendix and the script bridge them.
- **Hypothesis-driven.** State the answer you expect and test it with evidence; change the answer, not the
  evidence, when they disagree.

## 10. The speaker script

When the person asks for a script, or when you improve a reference deck, write one for every slide in
the deck's `script` field. The build writes it to `<file>-script.txt` beside the PDF and into the
PowerPoint speaker notes.

- **Spoken, not written.** Short sentences, the way the presenter would say them. Conversational and
  professional.
- **Per slide:** the point first (in other words than the title), then the evidence that matters, then
  the transition to the next slide. Two to five sentences for most slides.
- **Add, do not repeat.** The script says what the slide leaves out: the context, the example, the "so
  what" for this audience. It does not read the slide aloud (the redundancy principle).
- **Timing.** About 130 to 150 words a minute; the build estimates each slide's time at 140. A main story
  of 15 to 20 slides fits a 30 to 40 minute slot with time for questions.
- **Cues.** Mark demo moments and pauses in brackets: "[Open the Reconciliation prototype]".
- **Questions.** Where a hard question is likely, add one line on the answer and the appendix slide
  that supports it: "If asked about migration: A7."
- **Appendix slides** get a one-line script saying when to use them.
- The same rules as the slides: no invented figures, illustrative figures named as such, no em dashes.

## 11. Working from a reference deck

When the person gives a deck, a document or notes to work from, ask which job it is (`workflow.md`):

**Strictly by the reference.** Keep the reference's structure, order, slide count and wording as far as
the house style allows. Change only what the Praisindo design system and the slide guidelines require:
the shell, type, colours, layout within the frame, and copy fixes the rules demand (sentence case, no em
dashes or middle dots). List any improvement you would make as a suggestion at hand-over; do not apply
it.

**Improve upon it.** Rebuild the storyline with these principles: answer first, action titles,
horizontal and vertical logic, one message per slide, main story and appendix, the ask at the end. Write
the speaker script. Keep every fact and figure; move detail to the appendix rather than dropping it.
Never invent data, clients, quotes or commitments; when the storyline needs something the source does
not have (an owner, a date, a decision), propose it as a question to agree and mark it as proposed. At
hand-over, list what changed in the structure, what moved to the appendix, what you added and that it
needs confirming.

In both cases, apply the setting (room or device) to type and density.

## 12. Checklist

- [ ] The governing thought fits one sentence, and the executive summary says it first.
- [ ] Read alone, the titles tell the story (horizontal logic).
- [ ] Every slide makes one point, and its body supports only that point (vertical logic).
- [ ] Titles are full-sentence claims the evidence supports.
- [ ] Each slide passes the three-second glance test.
- [ ] Type and density suit the setting (room or device); nothing a reader needs falls below the
      setting's minimum.
- [ ] Charts encode by position or length, highlight the point, and are labelled directly.
- [ ] Figures reconcile; units, periods, sources and illustrative status are stated.
- [ ] Meaning never depends on colour alone; contrast holds when projected.
- [ ] Main story fits the slot; detail is in an indexed appendix; nothing was dropped silently.
- [ ] The deck ends with the ask and next steps.
- [ ] The script, if wanted, adds to the slides instead of reading them, and its timing fits the slot.
